The best Scrivener alternatives for Markdown writers in 2026

Scrivener keeps a book in one place, and it does that well. But it’s a rich text editor that stores your work in a project of its own. If you’d rather write in Markdown and keep plain files, these six apps are the ones to look at. One of them, Grasmere, is ours. We’ve tried to be fair about the others, and about when to stay with Scrivener.

In brief

  • Zettlr, if you want a free, open-source editor with citations built in and you’re at home with Pandoc.
  • Obsidian, if your book grows out of a large body of linked notes and you’re happy to add plugins.
  • iA Writer, if you want the calmest page to draft on, on Mac, Windows and iPhone.
  • Ulysses, if you work across Mac, iPad and iPhone and want ebooks out of the box.
  • Typora, if you want an inexpensive, good-looking editor for one document at a time.
  • Grasmere, if you cite sources or send drafts to people who use Word, and you write on a recent Mac.
  • Scrivener itself, if you depend on the corkboard, Compile or its Windows and iOS versions.

Start with what Scrivener does

One Scrivener project holds the draft, the research, the notes and the synopses. The binder puts chapters in order, the corkboard and outliner show the shape of the book, and each document can have a status, a label and a target. Snapshots keep earlier versions. Compile takes the Draft folder and makes a PDF, a Word file or an ebook from it. Any alternative has to be measured against that.

Two things send Markdown writers looking elsewhere. Scrivener’s manual describes it as “fundamentally a rich text editor”, with Markdown arriving only at Compile, as MultiMarkdown. And a project is a folder of Scrivener’s own files, shown as a single package on a Mac, which the manual warns against editing by hand.

Scrivener runs on the Mac, Windows and iOS. A licence is $59.99 for the Mac or for Windows, sold separately, with 30 days of use to try it.

Zettlr: free, with citations built in

Zettlr is a free, open-source Markdown editor for the Mac, Windows and Linux. Your files stay in your own folders. It reads a reference library as CSL JSON, BibTeX or BibLaTeX, you cite with Pandoc’s [@key], and the bibliography is added on export in whichever CSL style you choose. Pandoc is built in for export.

For a book, you make a folder a project and choose which files go in and in what order. The list is yours to keep up as files are added or renamed. A full PDF needs LaTeX, which you install yourself. A Word file can be imported, with one setting for all its comments, not a review of each change.

Choose Zettlr if you want citations in any style at no cost, and you don’t mind looking after Pandoc and LaTeX.

Obsidian: a book that grows out of your notes

Obsidian keeps Markdown files in a vault, which is an ordinary folder, and runs on every platform including Android. The app is free, and paid Sync starts at $4 a month, billed yearly. Nothing else here matches it for linking notes and finding your way around thousands of them.

It isn’t built for manuscripts. On its own it exports a note as a PDF, and it has no way to assemble chapters and no citations. All of that comes from community plugins, such as Longform, which other people maintain. Plenty of books are written this way. It takes some assembly.

Choose Obsidian if the notes are the main thing and the book comes out of them. If you later want the manuscript tools without the plugins, Grasmere opens the same vault.

iA Writer: the calmest page

iA Writer is plain text with Markdown, in files you keep where you like, on the Mac, Windows, iPhone and iPad. It’s a single purchase for each platform, $49.99 for the Mac. Content blocks pull other files into one document, which is how its users compile the chapters of a book. It exports PDF, Word and HTML, and it can turn a Word file into Markdown. Its Authorship feature marks text you pasted in or took from an AI.

What it doesn’t offer, as far as its documentation goes, is the apparatus around a long project. There’s no corkboard, no citations and no way to bring a reviewer’s tracked changes back.

Choose iA Writer if drafting is the whole job and you want nothing else on the screen.

Ulysses: polished, across Apple devices

Ulysses runs on the Mac, iPad and iPhone and syncs between them. It organises a book as sheets in groups, with goals and deadlines, and exports PDF, Word and ePub. It’s the most finished app here for writing on the move.

Two things to know. It’s a subscription, $39.99 a year or $5.99 a month. And by default your texts live in Ulysses’s own library, written in Markdown XL, its own markup “inspired by” Markdown. It can work on folders of ordinary Markdown files, with limits its help pages list, such as no embedded local images. Its documentation doesn’t mention citations, and says it has no built-in collaboration.

Choose Ulysses if you write on an iPad or iPhone as much as a Mac, and a subscription doesn’t trouble you.

Typora: one document, beautifully

Typora is a Markdown editor for the Mac, Windows and Linux that hides the marks as you type, so the page looks finished while you write. It works on folders of your own files and costs $14.99 once, for up to three devices. PDF and HTML export are built in. Word, ePub and LaTeX need Pandoc installed.

It’s an editor more than a workshop. Its documentation describes nothing for putting chapters in order, combining them, or citing.

Choose Typora if you write articles and essays, not books, and you want them to look good as you go.

Grasmere: sources, review and a manuscript

This one is ours, so weigh what follows accordingly. Grasmere is a Mac app for long writing in plain Markdown files, a chapter each, in folders you choose. It’s built around the two jobs the others leave to plugins or to Pandoc.

  • Sources and citations. Save web pages, add papers by DOI and books by ISBN, or import a Zotero library. Cite by typing [@. On export, citations and the reference list are written in APA, Harvard, Chicago or MLA.
  • Review in Word. Send a chapter or the whole book as a Word file. When it comes back, each tracked change and comment appears beside your Markdown, to accept or reject one at a time.

Chapters are cards with a synopsis and a status, in an order you drag. The Publish stage makes a PDF in standard manuscript format or as a typeset book, or one Word document. It’s $14.99 once, after a 14-day trial, with no account.

Its limits are real. It needs macOS 26 or later, and there’s no iPhone, iPad or Windows version. It writes four citation styles, where Zettlr writes any. It doesn’t make ebooks. And it’s new, first released in October 2026, so it hasn’t the years behind it that the others have.

Choose Grasmere if your writing cites sources, or goes to a supervisor or editor who works in Word. There’s more in A Scrivener alternative in plain Markdown, including how to bring a Scrivener project across.

How to choose

Three questions settle most of it.

  1. Where do you write? On Windows or Linux, look at Zettlr, Typora and Obsidian. On an iPad or iPhone, Ulysses, iA Writer and Obsidian. Grasmere is for the Mac alone.
  2. Do you cite sources? Zettlr and Grasmere have citations built in. Obsidian gets them from plugins. With the rest you’ll add Pandoc yourself.
  3. Who reads your drafts? If they use Word and track their changes, Grasmere is the one here built to bring those changes back into your Markdown, one at a time.

And stay with Scrivener if what you love is the corkboard, collections and Compile. Nothing above replaces all three.

Questions

Can Scrivener itself write Markdown?

Up to a point. You can type MultiMarkdown into it and Compile will turn that into other formats. The editor is still rich text, and the project is still Scrivener’s own.

Which of these are free?

Zettlr is free and open source. Obsidian is free, with paid sync and publishing. The others have free trials.

Which keep my book as plain files?

Zettlr, Obsidian, iA Writer, Typora and Grasmere all work on Markdown files in ordinary folders. Ulysses does when you use its external folders. Scrivener doesn’t.
